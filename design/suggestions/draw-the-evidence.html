Not today

One finding shapes everything below: loads inside a session vary by a few percent — 52.5 to 55 kg is 4.5%. A bar chart to scale shows four bars of identical height; one normalised to the session's range shows a 2.5 kg step as a cliff. So none of these graphics encode kilos as height. They encode steps — one step is one of your increments — which is exact, legible, and can't lie.

Most complete · least restrained

The set with no evidence yet

Every graphic here is drawn from the session in progress, which means the first working set of an exercise has nothing behind it. That is the case where a sentence is the honest answer — one line, once, and it disappears the moment set 1 is logged and the chart has something to show. Rather than a weakness, this is what makes the rest defensible: prose where there is no evidence, evidence everywhere else.

Where I'd land

The step profile, and nothing else. It is the only one of the four that needs no new field, no cropped scale and no caption — a green dot for what you lifted, a ringed dot for what is next, a dashed line for where you started. It kills all three sentences and takes less room than the two it replaces.

The meter is the tempting one to add and the one I would hold. Capacity-left is modelled, not measured, and its real range is narrow enough that an honest scale barely moves while a useful-looking scale overstates. If the fatigue model's numbers get more trustworthy, it becomes the best graphic here — but that is a modelling decision, not a design one.

The dots are worth doing on their own schedule. They are the one mark that keeps a number the prose was carrying, and they only ever appear on sets where the floor actually fired — but they need completedRIR carried through to the view first.
