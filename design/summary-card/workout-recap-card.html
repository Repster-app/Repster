Repster · finish-workout sheet

Workout Recap Card

The card at the top of the “Workout complete” sheet is the last thing a lifter sees before the session closes. Right now it reports three numbers and buries the best one. A working exploration, most recent first: three summary screens built to a settled brief, three ways of signing the share card, and behind them the twenty-odd directions that got us there.

Every colour, radius, type size and spacing value below is lifted from DesignTokens.swift and the recapHero function in WorkoutSummarySheet.swift, so the mockups sit next to the real screen honestly. Static mockups, not a prototype.

Where it stands

Four things working against the card today

None of these is a bug. The card does what it was specified to do — it just spends its emphasis in the wrong places.

  • The only tinted tile is the least useful number. prominent: true is hard-coded onto Time, so the accent tile points at how long you were in the gym rather than at anything you did there. And the tint itself — accentSoft, blue at 10% over bgCard — resolves to about #212733 against #1F1F25. The one hierarchy signal in the card barely renders.
  • The PR line is the smallest thing on the card. A personal record is the single best outcome a session can have, and it sits below the tiles at .caption in textSecondary — visually beneath “Sets”.
  • No number has a baseline. 18 sets and 12,450 kg carry no verdict. The card reports the session without ever saying how it went, which is the one question a lifter has at that moment.
  • Edit occupies the card’s best corner for a rare action. A persistent pencil button sits top-right at the same weight as the date. Retitling a workout is something people do occasionally; the title itself can carry that affordance.

Why it matters more than it looksNotes and effort are dead UI today — notesSection and effortSection exist but are never rendered, while the save still passes both through. So this screen does not declutter the sheet, it restores two inputs nobody can currently reach.

And deloadReadiness is the only reader of perceivedEffort. If effort has been unreachable since the last redesign, that rule has been starved for as long. Worth checking against real data before anything else here.

On calories. HealthKitService.estimatedKilocalories is already a pure static function, so the number is free. It returns nil with no bodyweight logged and its toggle defaults to off, so it stays a quiet line rather than a fourth tile that would leave a hole. And being MET × bodyweightKg × hours at a fixed 3.5, it is duration wearing a different hat — heavy triples and light curls for 64 minutes give the same figure. The fix is named in the file: scale by perceivedEffort, which the details screen makes possible for the first time.

On calories — it already exists, and it is weaker than it looks. HealthKitService.estimatedKilocalories is a pure static function you can call today: kcal = MET × bodyweightKg × hours, with the MET fixed at 3.5. Two consequences worth designing around rather than discovering later.

It is frequently absent. The estimate returns nil with no bodyweight logged, and the toggle that produces it defaults to off. The code comments are explicit that substituting a population-average weight was considered and rejected — “an absent number is better than a wrong one”. So a four-up stat row will show three cells for a lot of users, which is why V2 puts calories on its own quiet line instead of promoting it to a peer.

And it is duration wearing a different hat. Because it is bodyweight times hours, a 64-minute session of heavy triples and a 64-minute session of light curls produce an identical number. Sitting it next to sets and volume implies it responds to how hard you trained, and it does not. The file already names the fix — scaling the MET by perceivedEffort, between about 3.5 and 6.0 — which is a neat argument for S3: ask for effort, and the calorie figure stops being a restatement of the clock.

Where I’d go

Build the details screen first

Option A fixes three of the four defects with data that already exists. It moves the emphasis onto the best thing in the session, makes the PR the largest element instead of the smallest, and frees the corner Edit was occupying. Nothing new has to be computed or stored.

Option B is the same idea plus a baseline — and it is the only option that answers “how did that go?” directly. But it costs the most, and its degraded state, when there is no comparable previous session, is essentially Option A. That ordering is convenient: ship A, and if the deltas prove worth the plumbing, B becomes a band added to a card that already has the right shape.

Option C is the one I would hold. It is the prettiest of the four and the quickest to read, but it sits directly above a list of the same exercises, and a muscle-group split computed from three exercises is a weak signal presented with a lot of confidence. It would earn its place on a weekly summary far better than on a single session.

Option D is a real answer, not a fallback. If the read is that lifters want their numbers and not a moment, it is the most robust card here: every figure gets bigger rather than smaller, and it survives imperial units, long locales and a 30-set session without a single line of layout defence.

Of the five further out, E is the real contender — and it is not really a rival to A so much as its filling. A settles the card’s shape: one band that carries the single most meaningful thing about the session. E settles what belongs in that band once the engine can say it. Same layout, better sentence: a PR when there is one, what your estimates did when there is not.

H is the same bet with the roadmap attached. If Repster Coach ships, the recap is the most natural place it ever speaks — you have just given it a session to react to, and it has your attention for about four seconds. H subsumes E’s content into prose; the risk is entirely in the writing.

F and G are the genuinely risky ones, in different directions. F is the most beautiful thing on this page and the most likely to lie, because it renders sparse effort data with total confidence. G is safe to build and dangerous to choose: a single hero number becomes the thing people train toward. The 82 pt headline itself is not the risk — measured, it holds six digits inside the 342 pt column, so metric and imperial both fit.

I is worth answering even if it is never built. If the honest reason this sheet exists is that notes, effort and save-as-template needed a home, then the recap is decoration on a form, and every option above is decorating the wrong thing.

Of the three drawn to the scoping, J is the one I would ship — and its positive-only rule turns out to be more than tone. §1 names the hardest constraint in that document: the Coach cannot ship a card saying “your bench hasn’t moved in seven sessions” while the wand is the reason it hasn’t. A line that only ever speaks on the upside steps around that entirely, which means J can ship before the engine program lands rather than behind it. K and L both wait on something — a confirmed goal, or a decision about stance.

On the share cards: Share A is the spec, and Share B is what I would test against it. A is right about the session it is designed for, but most sessions set no records, and B3’s fallback for those days is “use the workout title”. B is a card that works on an ordinary Tuesday, which is where the volume of shares actually lives. Share C is not really a third option so much as a test the other two have to pass.

One thing worth stating plainly, because it reads like a contradiction: the Coach scoping says social is a settled no, and that is about in-app social — feeds, friends, following. Exporting an image the lifter posts themselves is the opposite of that, and R5 of the same document specifies the share card as a home for the review. The two are consistent.

And a change of mind, after twenty-one of these. Nearly every direction on this page is the same move: a hero block, then a meta line, with the argument only ever about what goes in the block. That is a lot of variation inside one assumption — that the recap card is the thing that needs fixing. The evidence says otherwise. R4 of your own scoping already says the sheet should carry less, not more, and counts the six things it asks for at the moment someone wants to leave. No card treatment touches that count.

So the last board is the one I would actually work from. Pick a screen structure first — is this a form, a review, or a moment? — and the card’s job falls out of the answer. S1 is the cheapest honest version and A’s shape drops straight into it. S3 is the one I find most interesting, because it is the only direction that keeps the engine’s inputs alive while cutting what the screen asks. S2 is the one worth arguing about, since it questions whether finishing should be a decision at all.

V2 is the one I would build. It carries everything on your list, the chip row answers “which lifts did I PR in” at a glance in a third of the height of a list, and it is the only one of the three that does not change shape when a session has nine exercises instead of five. V1 shows more and pays for it — it already only just fits.

Analyse with Coach solves a problem I raised two rounds ago. R4 caps the sheet at one positive-only line because a verdict handed to a tired lifter reads as a grade. A button is not a verdict: the user asks for it. So the Coach can say the sharper things — a stall, a session-order cost, an honest projection — on the screen the tap leads to, and the sheet itself stays within the rule. That also gives the parked coaching tiles a real front door rather than a destination nobody visits.

One caution on Discard. V2 and V3 put it beside Save as a full button; V1 keeps it as a text link underneath. There is already a confirmation alert behind it, so a misfire is recoverable — but the pair-of-buttons layout is the one where a tired thumb picks wrong, and it is worth knowing that is the trade being made rather than discovering it in a support email.

On the share cards: the brand bar is the only one of the three that survives being screenshotted, cropped and reposted, which is how these actually travel. If the point of the card is attribution rather than decoration, that is the one to test — and it is the one that most looks like an ad, so it is a genuine bet rather than a free win.

V4 is the answer to the brief as stated — the chip row was the wrong trade once you said you wanted the lifts listed, and the list costs less than V1 suggested because dropping the amounts takes a row from 35 px to 30. The result clears its frame by about 75 px, which is the margin a nine-exercise session needs.

The details screen is where the sheet’s real cost went. Four asks moved off the recap and onto their own screen, which is what makes the front sheet readable — but they are still four asks, and the honest version of this design decides whether all four survive. Effort has one reader; suggestion feedback has one; save-as-template and notes have none but the user. That is the list to argue with next.

The plan is written up at SUMMARY_SCREEN_IMPLEMENTATION_PLAN.md, alongside the other scoping docs. Short version: six small packages for the screen (W1–W6) that add up to about one focused pass, and the share card (W7) as its own piece of work, since nothing exists for it yet — there is no ImageRenderer and no ShareLink anywhere in the codebase.

Do the details screen first. Not because it is the biggest, but because it is the one that unblocks something already shipped: deloadReadiness reads perceivedEffort and nothing else does, and effort is currently unreachable. The first thing worth doing is not code at all — check whether real workouts have carried an effort value since the last redesign. That decides whether this plan opens with a bug fix or a feature.
