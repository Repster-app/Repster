# Instagram science content — sourced literature

Researched 2026-09-28 for an Instagram series sharing lifting-science takeaways
(carousels / single cards, literature-led rather than product-led). Nothing published.

Scope of the search: peer-reviewed work that is **recent (2024–2026)** and **notable in the
science-based lifting community** — i.e. papers that community figures (Zourdos, Schoenfeld,
Nuckols, Milo Wolf, Refalo, Israetel, Stuart Phillips) authored or actively discuss.

Every entry was checked against the journal, PubMed, or the publisher's own page on
2026-09-28. Where a detail was not verified it says so explicitly. `[brackets]` = only you
can decide it.

**Do not paraphrase past the caveats.** This audience checks citations, and the reach comes
from being the account that gets it right, not the account that gets it loud.

---

## How to credit on the slide

- Slide: author surname + year + journal. Caption: the DOI. That is what makes it checkable.
- **Figure reuse:** PeerJ, Frontiers, Sports Medicine – Open, JISSN and Scientific Reports
  entries below are open access under CC BY — their figures can be reused *with attribution*.
  Sports Medicine (Springer, entries 2/3/8), MSSE (entries 1, 16) and Sport Sciences for
  Health (entry 6) are **not** CC BY; redraw the data yourself rather than screenshotting.
- Tagging the authors is worth doing — Milo Wolf, Martin Refalo and Greg Nuckols regularly
  reshare accurate coverage of their own work.
- Repster tie-in exists naturally for supersets (8), proximity-to-failure/RIR (3, 4) and
  rest duration (7). Resist making the science post an ad; the tie-in is a caption line at
  most.

# TIER 1 — the headline papers

## 1. ACSM position stand on resistance training (first update since 2009)

Phillips SM et al. "American College of Sports Medicine Position Stand. Resistance Training
Prescription for Muscle Function, Hypertrophy, and Physical Performance in Healthy Adults:
An Overview of Reviews." *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*, 17 March 2026.
PubMed 41843416. Announcement: https://acsm.org/resistance-training-guidelines-update-2026/

- Evidence base: 137 systematic reviews, >30,000 participants. Lead author Stuart Phillips
  (McMaster).
- **Strength:** heavier loads, ~80% 1RM, 2–3 sets per exercise.
- **Hypertrophy:** ~10+ sets per muscle per week; loads anywhere from 30–100% 1RM worked,
  *provided sets ended close to failure*.
- **Power:** moderate loads (30–70% 1RM), intent to move fast on the lifting phase.
- **Frequency:** all major muscle groups at least twice weekly is the primary driver.
- Training to failure, machines vs free weights, and complex periodization did **not**
  consistently change outcomes for the average healthy adult. Bands, bodyweight and
  home-based work produced marked benefits.
- Post angle: "The first official update in 17 years, and the headline is: the biggest jump
  is from nothing to something."
- Caveat for the slide: these are population guidelines for *healthy adults*, not
  optimisation advice for advanced lifters. Don't let the "failure doesn't matter" line
  contradict entry 3 without explaining the difference (see entry 3's note).

## 2. Volume and frequency dose-response

Pelland JC, Remmert JF, Robinson ZP, Hinson SR, Zourdos MC. "The Resistance Training Dose
Response: Meta-Regressions Exploring the Effects of Weekly Volume and Frequency on Muscle
Hypertrophy and Strength Gains." *Sports Medicine*, 2026 (online ahead of print late 2025).
DOI 10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w. PubMed 41343037.
Free preprint: https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460

- 67 studies, 2,058 participants. Posterior probability that more volume means more gains:
  100% for both hypertrophy and strength.
- **Volume:** hypertrophy keeps climbing with only modest diminishing returns; strength
  shows much sharper diminishing returns.
- **Frequency:** once weekly volume is equated, frequency has negligible effects on
  hypertrophy but consistently identifiable benefits for strength.
- **The best visual in the whole list:** the *fractional* set-counting method fit the data
  best — a set counts 1.0 if it directly trains the measured muscle, 0.5 if indirect. So
  bench press counts as half a set for triceps. Draw this.
- Post angle: "Are you counting your sets wrong? The best-fitting model says a bench press
  is half a triceps set."
- Caveat: I did not verify a gains-per-set number; the abstract reports slopes and
  probabilities, not "X% per set". Don't invent a per-set figure.

## 3. How close to failure do sets need to be

Robinson ZP et al. "Exploring the Dose–Response Relationship Between Estimated Resistance
Training Proximity to Failure, Strength Gain, and Muscle Hypertrophy: A Series of
Meta-Regressions." *Sports Medicine* 2024;54(9):2209–2231. DOI 10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2.
PubMed 38970765.

- Hypertrophy increases as sets end closer to failure, non-linearly — **the slope tapers off
  past about 2 reps in reserve (RIR)**.
- Strength: negligible relationship. Every best-fit strength model's confidence interval for
  RIR contained zero.
- Post angle: "Effort matters for size. It barely matters for strength."
- **Reconciling with entry 1:** ACSM says failure isn't required; this says closer to failure
  grows more muscle. Both are satisfied by "get close, don't grind to failure". That
  reconciliation is itself a strong post.

## 4. Failure vs RIR, trained lifters, controlled trial

Refalo MC et al. "Similar muscle hypertrophy following eight weeks of resistance training to
momentary muscular failure or with repetitions-in-reserve in resistance-trained
individuals." *Journal of Sports Sciences*, 2024. DOI 10.1080/02640414.2024.2321021

- Quadriceps hypertrophy was similar whether sets went to momentary failure or stopped with
  reps in reserve, in already-trained lifters.
- Use as the applied companion to entry 3 — meta-regression plus a controlled trial in
  trained people is a much stronger carousel than either alone.
- Related from the same group, if you want a deeper cut: the proximity-to-failure scoping
  review, and the 2023 neuromuscular-fatigue trial in trained males and females.

# TIER 2 — strong carousel material

## 5. Lengthened partials vs full range of motion (trained lifters)

Wolf M et al. "Lengthened partial repetitions elicit similar muscular adaptations as full
range of motion repetitions during resistance training in trained individuals." *PeerJ*
2025;13:e18904. DOI 10.7717/peerj.18904. PubMed 39959841. **CC BY.**

- 25 resistance-trained participants (19 men, 6 women; 4.9 ± 4.1 years experience), 8 weeks,
  2 supervised sessions/week, within-participant design (one limb per condition).
- Upper-body exercises: machine chest press (flat and incline), dumbbell row, cable
  pulldown, overhead triceps extension, pushdown, supinating curl, Bayesian curl.
- Muscle thickness and 10RM improvements were **similar** between lengthened partials and
  full ROM. Bayes factors 0.16–0.39 = moderate support for equivalence.
- Authors' practical line: lifters chasing growth should emphasise the stretched position,
  via either full ROM or lengthened partials.

## 6. Long vs short muscle length (meta-analysis)

Strey CBW, Irigoyen, McMahon, Pinto. "Muscle hypertrophy from partial repetition at long vs.
short muscle length: A systematic review and meta-analysis." *Sport Sciences for Health*,
online 5 January 2026.

- 8 studies. Partials at **long** muscle lengths beat partials at short lengths overall
  (ES 0.283, p = 0.036), with the advantage biggest in distal regions (ES 0.433, p = 0.048)
  and present centrally (ES 0.276, p = 0.028). Consistent across quadriceps and biceps.
- Post angle: "Don't skip the bottom half of the rep."
- Caveats: only 8 studies, effects are small-to-modest, and one secondary source noted the
  average muscle-length difference between "long" and "short" conditions was only ~22%.
  Pair with entry 5 so the message is "emphasise the stretch", not "partials beat full ROM".

## 7. Rest between sets

Singer A, Wolf M, Generoso L et al. "Give it a rest: a systematic review with Bayesian
meta-analysis on the effect of inter-set rest interval duration on muscle hypertrophy."
*Frontiers in Sports and Active Living*, 14 August 2024. DOI 10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789.
**CC BY.**

- Small hypertrophy benefit to resting **more than 60 s**, likely mediated by preserving
  volume load. **No appreciable further difference beyond 90 s.**
- Limb measures (arm/thigh) tended to favour longer rests; whole-body measures sat near zero.
- Post angle: "Your rest timer only has one job: get you past 60 seconds."
- Repster tie-in is obvious here; keep it to a caption line.

## 8. Supersets

Zhang X, Weakley J, Li H, García-Ramos A. "Superset Versus Traditional Resistance Training
Prescriptions: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis Exploring Acute and Chronic Effects on
Mechanical, Metabolic, and Perceptual Variables." *Sports Medicine*, 4 February 2025.
DOI 10.1007/s40279-025-02176-8. PubMed 39903375.

- Chronic adaptations — maximal strength, strength endurance, hypertrophy — were **comparable
  to traditional sets, in less time**.
- **The nuance that makes the post:** agonist–antagonist pairings (e.g. biceps/triceps)
  maintain volume load; pairing two exercises for the *same* muscle compromises it.
- Also similar post-exercise hypotension to traditional training.

## 9. Sex differences in hypertrophy

Refalo MC, Nuckols G, Galpin AJ, Gallagher IJ, Hamilton DL, Fyfe JJ. "Sex differences in
absolute and relative changes in muscle size following resistance training in healthy
adults: a systematic review with Bayesian meta-analysis." *PeerJ* 2025;13:e19042.
DOI 10.7717/peerj.19042. PubMed 40028215. **CC BY.**

- **Absolute** muscle-size increases slightly favoured males (SMD 0.19, 95% HDI 0.11–0.28).
- **Relative** increases were similar between sexes (0.69% difference, 95% HDI −1.50 to 2.88
  — interval crosses zero).
- Authors' conclusion: females have similar hypertrophic potential to males, particularly
  relative to baseline.
- Post angle: "Women build muscle at the same rate, relative to where they start."

## 10. Training breaks

Halonen EJ et al. "Does Taking a Break Matter — Adaptations in Muscle Strength and Size
Between Continuous and Periodic Resistance Training." *Scandinavian Journal of Medicine &
Science in Sports*, 2024. DOI 10.1111/sms.14739.
Plain-language summary: https://www.jyu.fi/en/news/breaks-in-resistance-training-do-not-impair-long-term-development-in-strength-and-muscle-size

- 55 healthy **untrained** participants (32 ± 5 y). Periodic group: 10 weeks training →
  10 weeks detraining → 10 weeks training. Continuous group: 10 weeks nothing → 20 weeks
  training.
- No group differences in leg press or biceps curl 1RM, vastus lateralis or biceps brachii
  cross-sectional area, or countermovement jump.
- Regain was fast: pre-break levels were back within ~5 weeks of retraining. 30 weeks with a
  break matched 20 weeks continuous.
- Post angle: "A 10-week break cost them nothing but time."
- **Caveat that must be on the slide:** untrained participants. Do not extend this to trained
  lifters without saying it's an extrapolation.

## 11. Deloads — two studies that disagree in an interesting way

**(a)** Coleman M, Burke R, Augustin F, Piñero A, Maldonado J, Fisher JP, Israetel M,
Androulakis Korakakis P, Swinton PA, Oberlin D, Schoenfeld BJ. "Gaining more from doing
less? The effects of a one-week deload period during supervised resistance training on
muscular adaptations." *PeerJ* 2024;12:e16777. DOI 10.7717/peerj.16777. **CC BY.**

- A one-week deload at the midpoint of a 9-week programme **negatively influenced lower-body
  strength** measures (isometric and dynamic both favoured continuous training), with **no
  effect on hypertrophy**, power or local muscular endurance. Slight psychological edge to
  continuous training on a readiness-to-train questionnaire.
- `[unverified]` I did not confirm exactly what the deload week consisted of (complete
  cessation vs reduced volume). Check the methods before you state it on a slide.

**(b)** "Effects of deload periods in resistance training on muscle hypertrophy and strength
endurance in untrained young men using a randomized within subject design." *Scientific
Reports*, 24 February 2026. DOI 10.1038/s41598-026-40612-5. PubMed 41730991. **CC BY.**

- 19 untrained young men, within-subject (limbs randomised to conditions), 8 weeks.
  Continuous: unilateral leg extension + biceps curl 2×/week, 6–8 sets, 8–12RM. Deload: in
  weeks 4 and 8, once per week at 2 sets.
- Similar significant increases in muscle thickness and 10RM in both conditions; no
  time × condition interactions; between-condition effects small.
- Post angle: "Deloads don't buy you extra gains. They also don't cost much. Take one when
  you need one, not because the calendar says so."

## 12. Drop sets, rest-pause and other "advanced" systems

Tsartsapakis I, Zafeiroudi A, Kouthouris C. "Effects of Advanced Resistance Training Systems
on Muscle Hypertrophy and Strength in Recreationally Trained Adults: A Systematic Review and
Meta-Analysis." *Journal of Functional Morphology and Kinesiology*, 2026. PMC12922048.

- 24 studies (20 in the meta-analysis). Systems covered: rest-pause, drop sets, cluster sets
  / rest redistribution, tempo control, velocity-based training, eccentric overload.
- **Hypertrophy:** small and non-significant (g = 0.046) — no collective advantage over
  traditional sets when volume and effort were matched. Only rest-pause showed a modest edge.
- **Strength:** moderate and significant advantage (g = 0.351), largest for velocity-based
  training and eccentric overload.
- Authors: use them for goals, constraints and preference, not for superior growth.
- **Caveat:** MDPI journal, lower profile than everything else on this list. Fine to post,
  but expect someone in the comments to raise the journal. Don't lead the series with it.

# TIER 3 — nutrition

## 13. Creatine

Ashtary-Larky D, Mohammadi S, Hajizadeh L et al. "Creatine supplementation and resistance
training: a comparison between novice and experienced lifters — a systematic review and
dose-response meta-analysis." *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*,
2025. DOI 10.1080/15502783.2025.2586523. PMC12777911. **CC BY.**

- 61 controlled trials, 1,457 participants. Fat-free mass **+1.39 kg** (95% CI 1.07–1.70,
  p < 0.001); body mass +0.89 kg (0.76–1.01).
- Trained +1.82 kg vs untrained +1.23 kg FFM — ~50% larger in trained lifters, but the
  difference was **not statistically significant**. Don't headline it.
- No significant effect on fat mass, body-fat percentage or BMI. Non-linear dose
  associations for body mass and BMI.
- **Caveat that belongs on the slide:** fat-free mass includes water. Creatine draws water
  into muscle, so this is not 1.4 kg of contractile tissue.

## 14. Plant vs animal protein (12-week training trial)

Santini et al. "Similar effects between animal-based and plant-based protein blend as
complementary dietary protein on muscle adaptations to resistance training: findings from a
randomized clinical trial." *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*,
2025. DOI 10.1080/15502783.2025.2568047. PMC12509290. **CC BY.**

- 44 healthy young untrained men, 12 weeks, 22 per group. 45 g/day of supplemental protein as
  three 15 g doses with meals — soy/pea blend vs whey. Total intake ~1.7–1.8 g/kg/day.
- Whole-body lean mass: **+2.4 kg (plant) vs +2.5 kg (whey)**. Vastus lateralis CSA +0.9 cm²
  vs +1.3 cm². Leg press ~+64 kg vs ~+63 kg.
- Post angle: "Same total protein, same result."
- Caveat: untrained participants; the supplement was complementary to an already adequate
  diet, so this is not a claim about low-protein vegan diets.

## 15. Protein source and meal distribution (mechanistic)

Burd NA lab. "Impact of Vegan Diets on Resistance Exercise–Mediated Myofibrillar Protein
Synthesis in Healthy Young Males and Females: A Randomized Controlled Trial." *Medicine &
Science in Sports & Exercise*, September 2025. DOI 10.1249/MSS.0000000000003725

- 40 physically active participants (28 M, 12 F; 25 ± 4 y), 9-day resistance training
  intervention, four arms: omnivorous or vegan × balanced or unbalanced protein
  distribution. Intake 1.1–1.2 g/kg/day.
- Balanced = five meals at ~20% of daily protein each. Unbalanced = three meals at ~10%,
  30%, 60%.
- Integrated myofibrillar protein synthesis over 9 days: **no significant difference** by
  diet pattern or by distribution.
- **Two caveats, both slide-worthy:** (1) it measured protein synthesis over 9 days, not
  actual muscle growth; (2) it was funded by the Beef Checkoff — which makes a
  vegan-favourable null result *more* notable, and saying so pre-empts the comment.
- Note the intake is 1.1–1.2 g/kg/day, below typical lifter recommendations. Don't present
  this as "distribution never matters at any intake".

## 16. Bonus — stretching for growth

Warneke K et al. "Effects of Chronic Static Stretching on Maximal Strength and Muscle
Hypertrophy: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis with Meta-Regression." *Sports Medicine –
Open*, April 2024. DOI 10.1186/s40798-024-00706-8. PubMed 38637473. **CC BY.**

- 42 studies, 1,318 participants. Small but significant effects: strength d = 0.30,
  hypertrophy d = 0.20.
- Dose matters: longer stretch durations, longer interventions and higher frequencies reached
  significance (d = 0.26–0.28); lower doses did not (p = 0.13–0.39).
- Post angle: "Stretching builds a little muscle — at doses nobody actually does." Good
  myth-adjacent post as long as the effect size is on the slide.

# Deliberately excluded

- **"2.4 g/kg protein beats 1.6 g/kg."** Circulating on blogs in 2025. I could not find a
  peer-reviewed source establishing it. Left out rather than softened.
- **Anabolic window claims.** The "timing matters less than total intake" position is well
  supported, but the strongest sources I found for it are older than the 2024–2026 window
  this list is scoped to. Entry 15 covers distribution with a current citation.
- **Muscle memory / proteomic retention.** Mechanistically interesting (there is a 2024
  bioRxiv preprint on retained proteomic memory) but **preprint, not peer-reviewed** — don't
  post it. Entry 10 makes the practical point with a published trial.
